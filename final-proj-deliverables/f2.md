# Problem Framing

## Domain

People often look for music with a particular feeling rather than a particular artist or genre. Someone preparing for a commute may want music that feels "hype"; someone studying late at night may want something "dreamy." These words are useful because they communicate an intention quickly. However, the core problem is a semantic gap: subjective adjectives do not map universally to specific audio features. One listener's "dreamy" might mean soft piano and slow vocals, while another's means layered synthesizers, echoing vocals, and a steady beat.

Listeners find music through streaming searches, generated playlists, and recommendations from friends. Friends are especially useful when someone wants a suggestion outside their usual rotation. Such exchanges typically happen through conversations and song links. A listener names a feeling, receives suggestions, and clarifies what they meant if the suggestions miss the mark. Sending a familiar song can help, but it does not identify which part of the song matters. A friend might notice its tempo when the listener actually means its vocal texture.

Though streaming platforms can personalize recommendations based on listening history, knowing which songs someone enjoys does not necessarily reveal which qualities of those songs matter to them. Similarly, when exchanging recommendations with friends, listeners struggle to explain what they mean by subjective descriptions. Even when providing an example song, the recipient may focus on different musical characteristics than intended. As a result, communicating musical preferences often requires repeated clarification and trial and error, whether searching through streaming platforms or asking friends for recommendations.

---

## Bad Situation: Two friends use the same word for different sounds

Maya is studying late at night and wants unfamiliar music with a "dreamy" sound. She has a few songs in mind: they have layered synths, distant-sounding vocals, and enough rhythm to keep her alert. She asks her friend Daniel, who often recommends music, to send her something dreamy.

Daniel associates "dreamy" with gentle acoustic guitar, slow piano, and quiet singing. His recommendations make sense to him, but they are too subdued for Maya. She listens to the first few suggestions, tells him they feel more "sleepy" than "dreamy," and asks for something with more energy. Daniel tries again with songs he considers upbeat, but their bright, prominent vocals also miss what Maya wanted.

Maya eventually sends two songs she already likes. Daniel recognizes similarities, but he cannot tell whether she wants their rhythm, vocals, production, or some combination. They exchange more text messages to clarify. Only after Maya identifies the airy vocals and layered synths does Daniel have enough context to make a more useful suggestion.

The cost is shared: Maya spends time evaluating and rejecting songs when she wanted to begin studying, while Daniel spends time guessing and revising recommendations. Both are willing to help, but the difficulty arises because the same word names different sounds for each of them. If the conversation becomes too laborious, Maya may settle for an old playlist, while Daniel may become less willing to suggest music.

## The explanation works once, then has to be rebuilt

A week later, Maya wants more songs with that same sound. Daniel is unavailable, so she asks another friend. She says "dreamy" and receives another set of soft, slow songs. Maya knows she has already worked out how to explain her preference, but the explanation is buried in a separate text conversation. She could forward her earlier messages, reconstruct the description, or send the same example songs again. Each takes effort, and none guarantees that her new friend will notice the musical qualities she cares about.

Maya has also changed her mind about one previous example: the melody fits, but the lyrics are distracting. A plain list of approved songs would not capture this qualification. Her new friend sees which tracks Maya likes, but not what each track demonstrates, what she wants to avoid, or which of her earlier judgments she has revised.

The problem repeats whenever Maya asks someone new, revisits the same feeling, or changes what she means. She may give up on asking friends for unfamiliar songs because explaining her taste costs more effort than listening to music she already knows.

## The common problem

**A song link preserves an example; it does not necessarily preserve the reason that example fits a listener's description.** A word such as "dreamy" is easy to send but open to interpretation. A playlist supplies examples but leaves recipients to infer which features matter. Conversations can resolve the ambiguity, but the resulting explanation is scattered across messages and must often be reconstructed. As a result, listeners may repeatedly spend time explaining preferences they have already clarified, making recommendations between friends more effortful and discouraging the discovery of unfamiliar music.

## Evidence

### Musical feelings are meaningful but not perfectly uniform
Cowen, Fang, Sauter, and Keltner (2020) studied emotional responses to 2,168 music excerpts with participants from the United States and China. They identified 13 broad dimensions of reported musical feeling, including "dreamy," and found patterns that were shared across the two groups alongside variation and blended emotional experiences.[1] Their work establishes that descriptions of musical feeling convey meaningful information, while not reducing every listening experience to a single fixed label.

### What the evidence does not yet establish

The scenario above is illustrative, not an observed interview. The research establishes that musical feelings and social sharing are worth studying, but it does not measure how often friends misunderstand a particular adjective or how much time they spend clarifying it. Complaints about repetitive algorithmic recommendations would not establish that interpersonal problem either. We need to observe real exchanges and test whether preserving an explanation is useful enough to justify the effort of creating one.

### Recommendations between people are a real part of discovery
Babul and colleagues (2025) analyzed person-to-person Spotify music links and found that engagement with a shared artist depended on social context, including the sender–receiver relationship and how well the music matched the recipient's taste.[2] This supports the importance of the social recommendation setting: what one person shares and who receives it affect whether a recommendation is useful. The study does not measure how often people disagree about words such as "dreamy" or the effort needed to clarify them. Those are the specific gaps our project needs to test.

## Workarounds and Comparables

### Workarounds

**Chat and song links.** Friends already clarify their requests through questions, examples, and timestamps. This is familiar and may be all an occasional request needs. When the listener makes the same request later, however, the explanation may be scattered across messages and need to be found or reconstructed.

**Shared notes and documents.** A listener can collect song links and explanations in a shared document; friends can comment or add suggestions. This is a credible way to preserve both examples and reasoning. The group must still maintain the structure and distinguish suggestions from examples the listener endorses. Whether a dedicated interface would save enough effort is an open question.

**Seed-track radio.** A familiar song can seed a playlist of related tracks without the listener needing musical vocabulary. It may quickly produce a useful result. But a single seed does not tell another person which passage or quality the listener meant to emphasize.

**Recommendation forums.** A detailed post can reach many listeners and produce unexpected suggestions. The requester must still explain their preference and sort through responses, and may have to repeat that explanation for a different audience.

### Comparables

**Prompted playlists.** Spotify's AI Playlist and Prompted Playlist accept natural-language prompts to generate playlists.[3][4] They may be sufficient for someone who wants an automated answer. Their documented workflow does not focus on maintaining a listener-authored explanation that friends can inspect and respond to.

**Shared listening.** Spotify Blend combines listeners' tastes, while Jam lets friends listen and add songs to a queue together.[5][6] These tools support shared discovery, but their documented purposes do not focus on preserving why a particular passage illustrates one listener's meaning of a word.

**Pandora's Music Genome Project.** Pandora describes expert analysis of musical attributes to support recommendations.[7] Its attribute system can help connect related music, but it is different from a listener's own editable explanation of a word. We do not assume its recommendations use only metadata or that listeners share a universal definition.

The opportunity is narrow: help an explanation that worked in one conversation travel to the next. A new tool is worthwhile only if that reuse offsets the effort of making and maintaining the explanation.

## Solution Sketch	

Sound It Out would let a listener build a small, personal vocabulary for the sounds they regularly try to describe. The vocabulary is represented by a Sound Wall: a collection of words such as "dreamy," "hype," or "nostalgic," each connected to songs and explanations that illustrate the listener's meaning. Unlike a playlist, which shows someone what a listener enjoys, a Sound Wall captures which qualities of those songs the listener wants others to recognize. The same song might illustrate "dreamy" because of its layered synths and "nostalgic" because of its production style; the entry makes that distinction explicit.

To begin, Maya could create "dreamy" using just one song she knows and a short explanation such as "airy vocals and layered synths." The app would not require her to classify her entire library before receiving value. A user starting from scratch only needs a single track to seed a word. Furthermore, she could pinpoint exactly what she means by specifying a timestamp (e.g., "the layered synths starting at 1:12") so Daniel knows exactly which part of the audio to focus on.

When Maya asks Daniel for suggestions, she does not send a bare adjective over text. Instead, she makes her "dreamy" entry public and sends its address. Daniel opens the link in his browser, sees her explanation, and listens to the embedded audio examples directly. He does not need to create an account to view it.

Crucially, the back-and-forth communication happens entirely within the app. Rather than texting a Spotify link back to Maya, Daniel signs in and submits a song suggestion directly through the Sound It Out interface. Maya receives this pitch and can privately evaluate whether it fits. A correction such as "great synths, but the vocals are too intense" remains attached to the suggestion within the app, rather than disappearing into a text thread. If Daniel's suggestion is a perfect fit, Maya can accept it and easily promote it to be a example on her Sound Wall; she can choose whether to share it.

Several weeks later, another friend asks Maya what kind of music she wants to discover. Rather than reconstructing her explanation of "dreamy" or searching through an earlier conversation with Daniel, Maya shares the same entry link, which now contains her original examples, Daniel's successful pitch, and any new distinctions she chose to preserve.

The key design question is whether the explanation is useful without becoming homework for either the creator or the recipient. If reading the Sound Wall requires Daniel to study a massive, overwhelming document, he might give up. The minimum useful interaction is therefore deliberately small: Maya creates an entry with one familiar song and a short explanation, then immediately has something meaningful to share. If Daniel recognizes that he has no fitting songs, he can say so without sending a list of guesses; whether this actually saves effort remains to be tested. The design succeeds only if this small initial investment makes subsequent requests easier than repeatedly explaining the same preference in chat.

Automated recommendations informed by these examples could be explored after the core communication workflow succeeds. The first version can use track links and provider-supported embeds, subject to availability and platform terms; it should not assume that short copyrighted audio excerpts may be copied freely.[8]

## Ethical Analysis

The ethical consequences arise from the same design choice that addresses the problem: Sound It Out would make personal interpretations of music persistent and shareable. That can help people understand one another, but it can also reveal private associations, pressure people to explain their taste, or turn subjective judgments into social scores. The following analysis considers immediate users, people affected indirectly, changing use over time, and the consequences of widespread adoption. Each response is proposed for the design rather than claimed as already implemented.

### Stakeholders
Listeners creating Sound Walls gain a way to express and reuse a personal definition, but risk disclosing memories, emotions, or preferences they did not intend to make public. Friends receiving requests or recommending songs gain context and may give more appropriate suggestions, but could feel obligated to participate or judged when their suggestions are rejected. People whose music taste appears in shared spaces may be affected even without creating an account, for example if a friend publicly characterizes their recommendations. Artists, composers, labels, and rights holders may gain attributed discovery when their work is shared, but can be harmed by unlicensed audio reproduction or misleading associations. People with disabilities can be excluded if the interaction depends on dragging icons, reading visually dense cards, or hearing clips without accessible alternatives. Future recipients and nonusers may encounter a shared entry long after its creator forgot it was accessible.

### 1. Privacy and the cost of making meaning portable
**Observation.** A tag such as "nostalgic" may appear harmless, but the explanation behind it might reveal a breakup, grief, identity, or a relationship. The more useful the entry is for communicating meaning, the more personal it may become. A shareable URL could be forwarded beyond the intended recipient, including to people who never sought out the listener's profile.
**Values and tension.** Rich context supports understanding and connection; limiting disclosure supports privacy and autonomy. Completely hiding examples would weaken the purpose of the Sound Wall, while publishing everything would expose more than the listener meant to share.
**Design response.** Make entries private by default. Let the creator share one word and selected examples, preview exactly what a recipient will see, and make the entry private again. A public address can be forwarded; it is not a secret link. Do not require publishing an entire wall to request a recommendation. The remaining cost is an extra sharing decision, accepted because the creator knows which associations are personal.

### 2. Honest feedback versus respect for the person recommending
**Observation.** A rejection is about whether a song fits a request, but a friend may experience it as a judgment of their taste. If the app displays public rejection counts, people may accept mismatched songs out of politeness, making the saved definition less accurate. This harm can extend to recommenders who never intended their suggestions to be publicly evaluated.
**Values and tension.** Honest corrections improve communication; kindness and social trust make people willing to recommend again. Public feedback could make the system more informative while discouraging participation.
**Design response.** Keep the listener's fit judgments private by default. Offer neutral feedback such as "not quite the vocal style I meant" rather than public scores, and let the listener choose whether to send an explanation. Do not rank friends by acceptance rate. The recommender may learn less from private feedback, but the listener is not forced to trade social comfort for an accurate wall.

### 3. Personal authority versus an imposed definition of musical taste
**Observation.** An app that automatically assigns a fixed meaning to "dreamy" could recreate the original failure: users would have to conform to the system's categories. Conversely, asking every user to invent detailed labels and explanations risks excluding people who know what they like only when they hear it. This may especially affect people unfamiliar with technical musical vocabulary.
**Values and tension.** User authorship supports expression, while suggestions and defaults can improve accessibility and reduce effort. Too much guidance may quietly turn one platform's vocabulary into the presumed correct one.
**Design response.** Allow entries to start from a known song or a word. Offer optional, editable prompts such as "Is it the vocals, instruments, pace, or something else?" without requiring musical terminology. Never overwrite a listener's interpretation with an automatically generated definition. This gives users help getting started while preserving their authority over what the entry means.

### 4. Consent and safety for unsolicited suggestions
**Observation.** A shared link could invite an unwanted suggestion containing slurs, disturbing lyrics, political messages, or unexpectedly loud audio. Even if a track was suggested with good intent, the listener should not have to hear it to reject it. If sharing becomes broadly accessible, strangers could also use the suggestion channel for harassment.
**Values and tension.** Open contributions increase discovery and social participation, but unrestricted submissions threaten safety, control, and the listener's willingness to share.
**Design response.** Let owners limit who can suggest, block specific contributors, remove suggestions, and report abuse. Show artist/title and available explicit-content information before playback; never autoplay a suggested clip. Provide a way to hide or reject a track without listening or giving a reason. Metadata and filters cannot catch every offensive or surprising song, so the listener's control remains necessary.

### 5. Accessibility: a sound-centered system should not require a single way of perceiving sound
**Observation.** A visually complex Sound Wall could exclude blind or keyboard-only users. An exclusively audio-based explanation may also be inaccessible to deaf or hard-of-hearing people, who may use lyrics, visual media, rhythm, or other available cues to discuss songs. Making audio essential to every step would prevent these users from participating in the core communication task.
**Values and tension.** Audio examples convey the intended sound efficiently for many listeners, but that efficiency must not come at the expense of equal participation.
**Design response.** Provide textual song information and user-authored explanations alongside playable examples. Use labeled controls, logical heading order, visible focus, keyboard navigation, and screen-reader testing in line with WCAG guidance.[9] The app cannot fully translate a listening experience into text for every user, but it can let people create, understand, and respond to an entry through more than one interaction mode.

### 6. Artists and rights holders: making recommendations does not grant permission to distribute recordings
**Observation.** Sharing a song in context may help an unfamiliar or independent artist reach a listener who appreciates it for a specific reason. But if the app stores and redistributes unlicensed clips, creators and rights holders bear costs even though they did not choose to use Sound It Out. Short excerpts are not automatically free of licensing restrictions.
**Values and tension.** Low-friction examples help listeners communicate, while attribution and control over recordings protect creators. A small authorized catalog may provide fewer choices than unrestricted uploads.
**Design response.** Attribute tracks and artists, use provider-supported embeds or links where permitted, and do not host unauthorized copies of commercial recordings. Treat playback restrictions honestly and provide a route to the original platform. Explore permissioned or open-licensed audio for any clips hosted directly. This protects third parties while preserving the core ability to reference a sound.

### 7. Changing taste: today's explanation can become tomorrow's incorrect assumption
**Observation.** A persistent definition solves the problem of forgetting, but persistence creates another risk: Maya's meaning of "dreamy" may change. A friend viewing an old entry could trust it precisely because it looks saved and deliberate. A tool that preserves yesterday's understanding too faithfully could reproduce the same misunderstandings it was built to prevent.
**Values and tension.** Continuity makes explanations reusable; revisability protects accuracy and personal agency. Keeping every change may help reconstruct a conversation but can preserve personal details indefinitely.
**Design response.** Make it easy to edit or delete examples and change fit judgments. Identify when a shared entry was last updated and let its creator make the entry private when the meaning has changed. Avoid keeping a public history of every former association. This sacrifices some historical reproducibility to keep the listener's current meaning authoritative.

### 8. Widespread adoption could turn voluntary explanation into a social expectation
**Observation.** If Sound Walls became common, a person might be expected to publish their taste before asking friends for recommendations. Friends could interpret refusal to share a wall as being uncooperative, and recommenders might stop asking questions because they assume the saved profile tells them everything. This would change the norm of music sharing, not merely increase the number of people exposed to an existing risk.
**Values and tension.** Shared vocabulary can reduce misunderstanding and create richer conversations, yet social pressure to document one's taste threatens spontaneity and the right not to explain oneself.
**Design response.** Allow individual entries to be viewed without an account or a complete profile. In the initial design, submitting an in-app suggestion requires an account; friends may also continue an ordinary conversation outside the app. Present a Sound Wall as optional context, not a requirement for participation. Make room for a friend to ask questions even when an entry exists. This may produce less structured data, but preserves the informal character of sharing music.

### 9. Discovery and cultural diversity versus reinforcement of a narrow taste
**Observation.** Reusing a precise definition can help an overlooked artist reach an interested listener. At scale, however, if suggestions only repeat the same examples or surface songs from already familiar artists, Sound Walls could reinforce established preferences and exclude unfamiliar styles. Artists whose work does not fit conventional labels may be especially affected.
**Values and tension.** Accurate fit honors the listener's request; exploration can expand the listener's musical horizons and improve opportunities for less familiar artists. Neither should be silently imposed.
**Design response.** Keep "fits my description" separate from "surprises me in a good way." Permit a listener to request suggestions that are more adventurous without revising the underlying meaning of a word. Do not equate popularity with fit. For the initial project, prioritize a transparent friend-to-friend suggestion workflow rather than claiming the app can algorithmically balance discovery and diversity.

### 10. Secondary uses of personal associations
**Observation.** An accumulated dataset of people's descriptions and song associations might interest researchers or advertisers. Such reuse could reveal cultural patterns, but it would involve people who supplied information to communicate with friends, not to join a study or marketing profile. Cross-linking public entries could infer relationships and emotional characteristics that users never intended to disclose.
**Values and tension.** Aggregate research might produce social benefit; meaningful consent and purpose limitation protect the original relationship between the user and the app.
**Design response.** Collect only what the workflow needs. Do not sell taste profiles, use private entries for unrelated training, or publish aggregate analyses without separate, informed permission and safeguards. Provide deletion and export controls. A future research use would require a new consent decision rather than being justified by the fact that the data already exist.

### Ethical direction
The central ethical choice is to treat a Sound Wall as a listener's editable explanation, not a public diagnosis of their taste. That choice supports the original goal: it makes one person's meaning legible to someone else without making the listener surrender control over the explanation, the audience, or the right to change their mind. Privacy, accessibility, considerate feedback, and respect for artists are therefore requirements of communicating meaning responsibly, not separate features added after the fact.
